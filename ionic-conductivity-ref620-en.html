01 · Equipment

Know the equipment: sandwich cell and cable

Identified from the two photos. Points the photos do not show clearly are listed below; check them against the actual parts before you assemble the cell.

Sandwich cell (stainless steel blocking electrodes)

  • Two stainless steel plates: the top plate has a tab sticking out on the left, the bottom plate has a tab sticking out on the right. The two plates are the two electrodes.
  • A white layer sits between them, most likely a PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) spacer or insulating sheet.
  • Two white nylon bolts with metal nuts clamp the stack. Nylon insulates, so current can pass between the two plates only through the sample.
  • Stainless steel does not react with Li+ or similar ions. Ions that reach the surface cannot cross the interface, hence the name "blocking electrode."General method
Take the cell apart to check what the white layer does. If it is a spacer with a hole in the middle, the sample sits in the hole, and the hole diameter and spacer thickness set A and L. If the white layer is the sample itself, L is its thickness after clamping.

Gamry cell cable

  • Lead colors: green, blue, white, two reddish ones, and black (with alligator clip).
  • Of the two reddish plugs, the brighter one should be the orange Counter Sense lead; it looks reddish in the photo. The wiring below puts both on the same plate, so it does not matter if you cannot tell them apart.
  • The plugs are 2 mm banana plugs. To clip onto the cell tabs, put an alligator clip back on each lead. The manual says every lead comes with a removable alligator clip.Manual Ch 6
  • The other end of the cable is a 25-pin connector. Plug it into the Cell Cable connector on the Reference 620 front panel and tighten the screws.

03 · Making films that fit

Making polymer electrolyte films of the right size

For conductivity, the size, thickness and flatness of the film directly decide how accurate L and A are. The steps below go in order: set the size, choose a method, make the film, punch, weigh, transfer, and cure in situ, with one figure per step. These are all general lab methods; for the exact temperatures, solvents and drying conditions, follow your group's recipes and procedures.

  1. First set the film size for the cell you will use, then decide how to make the film. General method

    • Sandwich cell: fill the hole of the PTFE spacer; the thickness equals the spacer thickness.
    • Coin cell: make the disc one size larger than the SS spacer (e.g., 16 mm spacer, 18–19 mm film), but not so large that it presses on the gasket, or the cell will not seal properly.
    • PP ring: fill the hole of the ring and scrape the film flush with the ring (see the PP film section).
    • Thickness: for conductivity measurements, make the film somewhat thick (e.g., 100–300 µm) so that Rb is larger and easier to measure. If the film is too thick for a coin cell, reduce the thickness or number of SS spacers so the spring still has compression after crimping.

  2. Choose the film-making method according to the state of the material. General method

    • Solution casting is the most common, but the solvent must be removed completely.
    • Hot pressing uses no solvent, and the thickness is set by the spacers; it suits systems that can melt or soften, such as PEO-based ones.
    • Liquid precursors (e.g., UV- or heat-curable gels) are cured directly in the hole, giving the best size match and contact.
    • For very soft, sticky materials that are hard to punch, filling the hole directly or melt-filling is easiest.

  3. Solution casting: calculate the amount of solution for the target thickness, pour it into a leveled PTFE Petri dish, leave the lid ajar so the solvent evaporates slowly, then vacuum-dry to constant weight. General method

    • Thickness ≈ solute mass ÷ (density × dish area); estimate it with the calculator below.
    • Evaporating too fast causes bubbles and a film that is thick at the edge and thin in the middle; punch from the uniform central part.
    • Residual solvent and moisture make the conductivity falsely high. Dry according to your group's procedures until the mass no longer changes; if needed, confirm by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA).
    • Handle solvents (e.g., acetonitrile) in a fume hood.

  4. Hot pressing: place the material between two release films with spacers of known thickness on both sides, heat above the softening or melting temperature and press flat, then cool under pressure before removing. General method

    • The spacer thickness sets the film thickness, the same idea as the PP ring in the PP film section.
    • Set the temperature for the material, without exceeding the decomposition temperature of the polymer or lithium salt; beware of burns.

  5. Punching: punch through the film together with the release films on both sides; cool very sticky films on a pre-chilled metal block until they harden, so the edges come out clean. General method

    • Use a punch, not scissors: scissors deform the edge and thin it locally.
    • Do not stretch the film while punching. If electrolyte sticks to the cutting edge, wipe it clean with solvent before punching the next disc.
    • Remeasure the diameter after punching; soft films shrink back.

  6. Weigh each disc, estimate the average thickness from its mass, and compare with the micrometer reading. General method

    • t = m / (ρ · A): gives the average thickness without touching the film, suitable for soft films that deform when pressed.
    • Estimate the density ρ from the formulation, or calibrate it with a film of known size and thickness.

  7. Transfer: handle the film together with its release film, place it on the SS spacer, level and center it, then slowly peel off the release film at a low angle. General method

    • If the film sticks to the release film and will not come off, cool it first, or switch to another release film such as PTFE sheet or silicone-treated PET film.
    • Use PTFE-tipped tweezers; metal tweezers can scratch or stick to the film.
    • Store finished films in the glovebox and assemble cells there as well, to avoid moisture uptake.

  8. In situ curing (for liquid precursors): drop the precursor into the hole of a PP or PTFE ring spacer placed on an SS spacer, scrape it level, cure with UV or heat, then add the top spacer and crimp. General method

    • UV does not pass through stainless steel, so cure with the cell open first and then add the top spacer; heat curing can be done after crimping.
    • Cure completely: residual unreacted monomer affects conductivity and stability.
    • The resulting film matches the hole size exactly and makes the best contact with the electrodes.
    • Wear protective eyewear when using a UV lamp.
    • For formulations with UV-crosslinkable groups or added plasticizer, see the subsection below for details.

03b · UV crosslinking & plasticizers

With UV-crosslinkable groups and a plasticizer

When the polymer carries acrylate or other UV-crosslinkable groups and a plasticizer must be added, plan two things in advance: when to add the plasticizer (once it is in, no more long vacuum drying), and how to keep UV curing clear of oxygen inhibition, insufficient cure depth and cure shrinkage. Everything below is a general method; for the exact formulation, initiator and dose, follow your group's procedures.

  1. Prepare the precursor: in the glovebox, protected from light, mix the crosslinkable monomer or oligomer, lithium salt, plasticizer, and photoinitiator, and stir until fully dissolved. General method

    • Dry the lithium salt and plasticizer first. Many plasticizers absorb water easily; dry them with activated molecular sieves.
    • Match the photoinitiator absorption wavelength to the UV lamp (e.g., a 365 nm lamp). A typical loading is about 0.5–2 wt% (relative to the crosslinkable components); follow the formulation literature.
    • If heating is needed to help dissolution, keep it mild to avoid premature crosslinking. Store the mixture protected from light and use it up soon.
    • Record the mass of each component as you weigh it; check the wt% with the formulation calculator below.

  2. Decide in advance when to add the plasticizer: once it is added, the material can no longer be vacuum-dried for long. General method

    • Solvent-casting route: cast the polymer and lithium salt and vacuum-dry to constant weight first, then add the plasticizer in the glovebox (dropwise or by soaking), and weigh to determine the actual content.
    • Solvent-free UV route: all components are already a liquid mixture; fill the hole or form a film and cure directly, with no drying needed, so the plasticizer content equals the formulation value. This route suits plasticized systems best.
    • Low-boiling solvents such as DMC and DEC evaporate quickly and are not suitable as plasticizers for conductivity measurements. EC, PC, succinonitrile (SN), glymes, and ionic liquids evaporate slowly but are still lost under prolonged vacuum heating.

  3. Remove bubbles: let the mixture stand or centrifuge it; if you must pull a vacuum, keep it short and gentle. General method

    • Bubbles become voids after curing, making both A and L inaccurate.
    • Vacuum also removes volatile plasticizer, so weigh before and after.

  4. Fill, cover, and cure: slightly overfill the hole with precursor, press a UV-transparent cover sheet onto the ring to squeeze out the excess, and cure by irradiating through the cover sheet. General method

    • Free-radical polymerization of acrylates is inhibited by oxygen, leaving a tacky surface. Cure under the inert atmosphere of the glovebox; the cover sheet also excludes oxygen.
    • For the cover sheet, use quartz or a film that transmits at the lamp wavelength (e.g., PET film at 365 nm; confirm transmission before use). PTFE sheet transmits poorly and is not suitable.
    • After curing, peel off the cover sheet, trim the excess outside the ring, then add the top spacer and crimp.
    • To make a freestanding film: sandwich the precursor between two cover sheets with spacers on both sides, cure, then punch.
    • Record the lamp wavelength, power, distance, and exposure time, and use the same dose for every batch. The UV lamp gets hot, and overly long exposure also evaporates plasticizer.
    • Wear UV-protective goggles and gloves: acrylate monomers readily cause skin sensitization.

  5. Check the cure: after the cover sheet is removed, the surface should be tack-free, the whole film cured, and flush with the ring. General method

    • Tacky surface: oxygen inhibition or insufficient dose. Cure under inert atmosphere, use a cover sheet, or extend the exposure.
    • Tacky bottom: the layer is too thick or the formulation absorbs too strongly (lithium salts and fillers both absorb light). Make it thinner, or cure in layers.
    • Gap at the edge: cure shrinkage. Fill another layer and cure again, or switch to a low-shrinkage oligomer.
    • For a quantitative check, use infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to see whether the acrylate C=C peaks (about 1635 cm⁻¹ and 810 cm⁻¹) have disappeared; or measure the gel content, i.e., the mass fraction left insoluble after soaking in solvent (plasticizer and lithium salt also dissolve out and must be subtracted).

  6. Monitor the plasticizer during measurement: measure at intervals at constant temperature, and σ should be stable. Weigh before and after the measurement. General method

    • σ keeps dropping: the plasticizer is evaporating or being squeezed out. Check the seal, or switch to a less volatile plasticizer.
    • Surface wet and glossy after disassembly: the plasticizer has exuded and may have flowed between the ring and spacer, making A and L inaccurate.
    • When reporting conductivity, state the plasticizer type and wt%, and the mass change before and after the measurement.

04 · Geometry

Inside the cell: where L and A come from

The accuracy of the conductivity depends mainly on how accurately L and A are measured; Rb itself is usually easy to read accurately.

  • L: measure the sample thickness with a micrometer, ideally after the cell is clamped, or use the spacer thickness (when the sample fills the spacer hole). Average several points.General method
  • A: the area where the sample actually touches both stainless steel plates. With a spacer that has a hole, use the hole area. If the sample is smaller than the electrodes, use the sample area; if it is larger, use the electrode area.
  • Units: L in cm (100 µm = 0.01 cm), A in cm². A disc 10 mm in diameter has A = 0.785 cm².
  • The sample must make good contact with the stainless steel, with no bubbles. Assemble moisture-sensitive samples, such as those containing lithium salt, in a glovebox and keep the cell sealed during the measurement.General method
  • After assembly, measure across the two tabs with a multimeter. A reading near 0 Ω means the plates are shorted directly (the sample is broken or the spacer is misaligned); reassemble.
05 · Wiring

Sandwich cell wiring: two-electrode connection

The sandwich cell has only two electrodes, so use the two-electrode connection from the manual: with no reference electrode, connect the white reference lead to the counter electrode.Manual Ch 6

  • Green (working electrode WE) + blue (working sense WS) → each with its own alligator clip, on the top-plate tab.Manual Ch 6
  • White (reference RE) + red (counter electrode CE) + orange (counter sense CS) → each with its own alligator clip, on the bottom-plate tab. Connecting the white lead to the counter electrode when there is no reference electrode is the two-electrode connection from the manual.Manual Ch 6
  • The orange lead is used only in ZRA mode, and the manual allows leaving it unconnected, but then its metal tip must not touch any other electrode. Connecting it to the bottom plate is the least trouble.Manual Ch 6
  • If the tab is too narrow for three clips: the red lead must clip directly onto the tab; white and orange can clip onto the metal part of the red lead's clip. Sense leads carry almost no current, so the extra contact point has little effect.General method
  • The black pin is the instrument's Floating Ground. In most cases leave it unconnected, with its metal tip not touching any electrode. For very small currents, put the cell under a metal shield (Faraday shield) and connect the black lead to the shield.Manual Ch 6

How to stack the five common cells

  • SS | electrolyte | SS: ionic conductivity (EIS) and electronic conductivity (DC polarization).
  • SS | PTFE | SS: blank cell for electronic conductivity, with thickness and area as close to the sample as possible.
  • Li | electrolyte | Li: Li+ transference number, Li symmetric cycling, critical current density (CCD). The electrolyte film must be slightly larger than the Li foils all around.
  • SS | electrolyte | Li: electrochemical stability window. SS is the working electrode; Li serves as both counter and reference electrode.
  • SS | separator + liquid | SS: effective conductivity of a liquid electrolyte; see PP film.
  • Assembly order (bottom to top): positive case → SS spacer → electrolyte film or separator wetted with liquid electrolyte → SS spacer → spring → gasket and negative cap. Finally, crimp with the crimper.General method
  • A: the area of the smaller of the spacer and the electrolyte film. Common spacers are 15.5–16 mm in diameter (A ≈ 1.9–2.0 cm²); use the size you actually have.
  • L: film thickness. Crimping and spring pressure squeeze a soft polymer film thinner, so measure it again after taking the cell apart. When you need a fixed thickness, put a PTFE ring spacer of known thickness around the film.General method
  • Blank cell: assemble a cell with no electrolyte, the two spacers touching directly, and all other parts the same. Its high-frequency resistance Rblank is the case, spring and contact resistance; subtract it in the calculation. This step matters when the sample resistance is only a few to a few tens of ohms.General method
  • Separator wetted with liquid electrolyte: what you measure is the effective conductivity, which is lower than that of the liquid itself because the porosity and tortuosity of the separator reduce it. To measure the conductivity of the liquid itself, use a conductivity meter or a conductivity cell with a known cell constant.General method

Assembly steps: from parts to crimping

The steps are the same for all five cells; only the top and bottom electrodes differ (see the stack figure above). Assemble cells that contain lithium or are moisture-sensitive entirely in a glovebox.

  1. Prepare one set of CR2032 parts and tools per cell; for cells containing lithium or moisture-sensitive materials, bring all parts and tools into the glovebox. General method

    • Parts: positive case, negative cap with gasket, two SS spacers (e.g., Ø16 mm), and one spring; add Li foil for lithium cells. Prepare 1–2 spare sets.
    • Tools: plastic or ceramic tweezers (never let metal tweezers touch the positive and negative sides at once), punch, micrometer, crimper, and CR2032 die.
    • Match the spacer thickness to the spring: first assemble an empty trial cell without film; proceed only if, after crimping, it does not rattle and is not crushed out of shape.

  2. Clean and dry the parts first, then bring them into the glovebox through the antechamber. General method

    • Clean spacers, cases, and caps ultrasonically in ethanol or isopropanol, then dry them; evacuate the antechamber according to the glovebox procedure.
    • The spacers are the electrodes: their surfaces must be flat and free of scratches and rust spots. Replace any faulty spacers.
    • Handle parts only with tweezers; do not touch electrode surfaces with your fingers.

  3. Punch the film and measure its thickness L and diameter d. General method

    • Use a punch one size larger than the spacer (e.g., Ø16 mm spacer, Ø19 mm film); a punch gives cleaner, burr-free edges than scissors.
    • Measure 5 points with the micrometer (center and around the edge) and average them as L; for soft films that deform when pressed, use the weighing method t = m / (ρ·A).
    • Lithium cells: punch the Li foil one size smaller than the film (e.g., Ø14–16 mm); if the surface is dull, scrape it gently until shiny, then press it flat onto the spacer. Record the Li foil diameter.

  4. Place the positive case open side up on a clean insulating mat; use tweezers to place the bottom spacer in the center of the case. General method

    • Li | Li: first attach the Li foil to the bottom spacer, lithium side up. SS | Li: place only the SS spacer at the bottom; the lithium goes on top (negative cap side).
    • An off-center spacer puts uneven pressure on the film and may touch the case wall.

  5. Use tweezers to place the film in the center of the bottom electrode, lowering it slowly from one side to push out bubbles without wrinkling. General method

    • The film must cover the entire bottom electrode, with no edges lifting or curling under the spacer; if the spacer is exposed, the top and bottom electrodes may touch directly.
    • Soft, sticky films: first place a PP or PTFE ring spacer, then put the film into the ring hole, or cure it in situ in the ring hole (see PP film).
    • Liquid electrolyte: after placing the separator, add a fixed volume with a pipette (e.g., a few tens of µL), the same for every cell, and record the volume.

  6. Place the top spacer on the film, aligned with the bottom spacer, then place the spring in the center of the top spacer. General method

    • Li | Li and SS | Li: attach Li foil to the top spacer, lithium side down, facing the film.
    • If the top and bottom spacers are misaligned, the effective area is smaller than the A calculated from the diameter.

  7. Put on the negative cap with its gasket and make sure it sits flat. Place the cell in the CR2032 die according to the crimper instructions, apply the set pressure, hold for a few seconds, then release. General method

    • Check before placing the cell in the die: if the cap is tilted or the gasket is squeezed out, disassemble and reassemble.
    • Use the same crimper and the same pressure for a series; the die must match the cell size.
    • If the cell sticks in the die, remove it with an insulated tool.

  8. Wipe, inspect, and label. General method

    • Wipe the case clean with a lint-free wipe dampened with isopropanol; do not use cells that are bulging, leaking, or have the gasket squeezed out.

07 · PP film

Using PP film in coin cells

Two kinds of PP (polypropylene) film are common in coin cells: microporous PP separator and dense PP film. They are used in completely different ways, so first work out which one you have. Everything in this section is general lab practice; the Gamry manual does not cover it.

First, tell which kind it is

  • Microporous PP separator: it is opaque white because its many micropores scatter light. Once ethanol fills the pores, that spot turns translucent. White color alone is not enough; only turning transparent with ethanol confirms it.General method
  • Dense PP film: transparent to begin with. Ethanol only spreads on the surface and evaporates, and the film itself does not change.
  • Both films are insulators and do not conduct ions on their own; a microporous separator conducts only when wetted with electrolyte.

Microporous PP separator: wet it with liquid electrolyte, measure effective conductivity

Sandwich the separator between two SS spacers to make an SS | separator + electrolyte | SS blocking cell. The spectrum usually shows only the low-frequency spike (the semicircle lies above 5 MHz); take R where the spike meets the real axis.

  1. Punch the separator into discs one size larger than the spacer (e.g., 16 mm spacer, 19 mm separator), dry them, and bring them into the glovebox. General method

    • The separator is larger than the spacers so that the edges of the two spacers cannot touch.
    • PP does not tolerate high temperatures. Dry according to your group's procedures, typically under vacuum at about 60 °C.
    • Handle with tweezers, not bare hands; oils from your hands impair wetting. The thin film picks up static easily; use antistatic tweezers or an ionizing fan.

  2. PP separators are thin (commonly 20–25 µm). Stack several layers, measure them together, and divide by the number of layers to get the single-layer thickness L1. General method

    • You can start with the thickness from the datasheet, but preferably verify it yourself.

  3. Assemble: positive case → SS spacer → PP separator → add electrolyte dropwise → SS spacer → spring and negative cap → crimp. General method

    • Use enough electrolyte to soak the whole separator, typically a few tens of µL; use the same amount for every cell in a batch.
    • Lay the separator flat and centered, without wrinkles or bubbles.

  4. After crimping, let the cell rest for a few hours (commonly overnight) before measuring. PP is hydrophobic, so electrolyte wetting takes time. General method

    • A fully wetted separator turns from white to translucent; you can open a trial cell to check.
    • Alternatively, measure EIS at intervals and take the value once the high-frequency intercept stops changing.
    • Ionic liquid, aqueous, or highly concentrated electrolytes may not fully wet PP, and the measured resistance will be too high; in that case, switch to an easily wetted separator such as glass fiber.

  5. Multilayer method: assemble cells with 1, 2, 3, and 4 separator layers, measure the high-frequency intercept R of each, and fit a straight line against the number of layers n. The slope is the resistance per separator layer; the intercept is the case and contact resistance. General method

    • This needs no blank cell, and the contact resistance is subtracted automatically.
    • Preferably assemble 2 or more cells for each layer count and average.

  6. Calculate the effective conductivity σeff from the slope. If you know the conductivity of the electrolyte itself, σ0, you can also calculate the MacMullin number NM = σ0 / σeff, which shows by what factor the separator (porosity, tortuosity) increases the resistance. General method

    • σeff is the effective value of "separator + electrolyte", not the conductivity of the electrolyte itself.
    • The calculator below does this directly.

09 · Procedure

Measurement steps

The example uses one temperature and one sample, with a schematic for each step. For a temperature series, repeat steps 5–8 at each temperature.

  1. Make sure the instrument is powered on and has passed its self-test, and that Calibrate Instrument has been run (steps in the Instrument calibration section). Manual Ch 5

    • If the sample impedance is very high (thick film, low conductivity), run the Low I DC calibration first; the manual notes that EIS on high-impedance samples is sensitive to it. Manual Ch 5
    • If the spectrum of a high-impedance sample shows phase spikes, run the cable capacitance calibration (20 kΩ calibration cell). Manual Ch 5

  2. Measure the sample thickness L with a micrometer at several points and average. Then determine the contact area A (see the L and A section). General method

    • For a soft polymer film, do not tighten the micrometer hard; use the ratchet to just touch the film, or you will compress it.
    • With a spacer that has a hole, L is the spacer thickness and A is the hole area.

  3. Assemble the cell: stack, from bottom to top, the lower plate, the spacer holding the sample, and the upper plate, then tighten the two nylon bolts alternately in several passes. General method

    • Do not overtighten: nylon threads strip easily, and the sample can be compressed or even pierced.
    • Assemble moisture-sensitive samples (e.g., containing a lithium salt) in the glovebox, and keep the cell sealed during the measurement.

  4. With a multimeter on the resistance range, measure between the two terminal tabs to confirm the two plates are not shorted, then wire the cell as in the Sandwich cell wiring section. General method

    • A normal reading is OL or in the megohm range and rises slowly: under DC, ions are blocked at the stainless steel surface, so the multimeter mainly sees this charging process.
    • A reading near 0 Ω means the two plates are in direct contact. Disassemble, check the sample and spacer, and reassemble.

  5. Control the temperature: put the cell in an oven or on a hot plate. After the setpoint is reached and stable, wait a while longer (typically 30–60 min) before measuring. General method

    • To record the temperature, use the type K thermocouple jack on the Reference 620 rear panel. No thermocouple is supplied with the instrument; provide your own type K thermocouple with a yellow mini plug. Manual Ch 7
    • The manual gives a thermocouple accuracy of about ±4 °C (after calibration), so for activation-energy calculations, preferably use the temperature from the oven or an independent thermometer. Manual Ch 7

  6. In Gamry Framework, select Experiment › G Electrochemical Impedance › Potentiostatic EIS and fill in the parameters as in the EIS parameters section.

    • The menu path shown follows a common Framework version; names may differ slightly between versions.
    • Enter 1 cm² for Area first so that you read the unnormalized Rb; use the actual area when calculating σ.

  7. Start the run. The software first measures the open-circuit potential, with the yellow Cell On light off; the light turns on when the signal starts being applied. Manual Ch 7

    • Do not touch the leads while the yellow light is on, or data quality will suffer. To change the wiring, wait until the experiment ends and the yellow light goes off. Manual Ch 7
    • The open-circuit potential of a symmetric stainless steel cell is close to 0 V. If the red Overload light stays on continuously, check whether a clip has come loose. Manual Ch 7

  8. Open the .DTA file in Echem Analyst 2, view the Nyquist plot, and read Rb, or fit an equivalent circuit (see the Predicted spectrum section).

    • Quick reading: take the real-axis intercept at the right end of the semicircle (where the spike starts) and subtract Rs.
    • More accurate: fit with Rs + (Rb ∥ CPE) + CPE and take the fitted Rb.

  9. Calculate σ as in the Calculating conductivity section. Preferably assemble 2–3 cells of the same sample and report the mean and standard deviation. General method

    • The values in the figure are examples, not measured data.

11 · Electronic conductivity

Electronic conductivity: DC polarization

Apply a small constant voltage across SS | electrolyte | SS. The ions are blocked at the stainless steel surfaces and gradually stop; the steady-state current that remains is the electronic current (Wagner DC polarization). A good polymer electrolyte conducts almost no electrons, so σe should be several orders of magnitude below the ionic conductivity. The instrument points (shielding, low-current calibration, keeping the leads still) follow the manual; the method itself is a general electrochemical method.

σe = Iss · L / (ΔV · A)
Iss
Steady-state current (blank subtracted), A
ΔV
Applied voltage, V
L, A
Same as for ionic conductivity, cm, cm²

  1. Confirm that Calibrate Instrument has been run, and run a Low I DC calibration on the same day (steps in the Instrument calibration section). Manual Ch 5

    • The steady-state current is in the pA–nA range and uses the 60 pA and 600 pA ranges; the offsets of these two ranges drift with time, temperature, and humidity.
    • For Low I DC, wire as shown in the software's Cell Connection Information window: green + blue clipped together inside the shield, white + orange + red clipped together outside the shield, and the black lead to the shield.
    • Cable capacitance calibration is not needed; it only affects high-frequency EIS.

  2. First estimate how large the current will be, to confirm that you need shielding and the low current ranges. General method

    • Right after the voltage is applied, the current is mainly ionic: I0 ≈ ΔV / Rb, often in the mA range.
    • Once the ions are blocked, only the electronic current remains: Iss = σe·A·ΔV / L. For σe of 10⁻¹⁰–10⁻¹² S/cm, this is only a few nA down to tens of pA.
    • The current spans 6–8 orders of magnitude, so set I/E Range to Auto.

  3. Make a blank cell: replace the electrolyte with an insulating PTFE film, assemble SS | PTFE | SS, and measure it once under the same conditions to get the background current Iblank. General method

    • Use the same holder, thickness, area, shielding, voltage, and time as for the sample.
    • The background current comes from the holder, cables, and instrument: the manual notes that imperfect insulators form shunt resistances, and moving leads also generate current. Manual Ch 9
    • Iblank should be much smaller than the sample Iss; if the two are similar, the sample result is not reliable.

  4. Assemble and wire the cell as for EIS, then place the cell in the Faraday shield and connect the black lead to the shield. Manual Ch 6, 9

    • Connect green + blue to one plate and white + red + orange to the other plate; route the two groups of leads separately.
    • If the cell is in a metal oven, the oven body can serve as the shield: connect the black lead to the oven body, but neither the electrodes nor the holder may touch it.
    • If either electrode of the cell is connected to earth ground, do not also ground the instrument chassis. Manual Ch 1, 6
    • Arrange and secure the leads and do not move them during the measurement: lead movement itself generates current noise. Manual Ch 9

  5. Stabilize the cell at the measurement temperature, wait at least 30–60 minutes before starting, and keep the temperature constant throughout the measurement. General method

    • Temperature changes make the ionic and background currents drift, so the steady-state value cannot be read accurately.
    • Keep the oven door closed to avoid air drafts and vibration; record the actual temperature.

  6. First measure EIS to get Rb and the ionic conductivity σion (parameters in the EIS parameters section), and confirm there is no short circuit. General method

    • If the spectrum is only a point near the real axis, the two plates are shorted; disassemble and reassemble.
    • Record Rb; step 11 compares it with the value after polarization.

  7. Choose 2–3 voltages well below the decomposition voltage, e.g., 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 V. General method

    • To find the decomposition voltage, run one slow LSV (e.g., 0.1–1 mV/s) on a separate SS–SS cell and note the voltage at which the current starts to rise clearly. LSV damages the sample, so do not use the cell intended for the electronic conductivity measurement.
    • On SS–SS you only get the total width of the stability window, not potentials vs Li/Li+.
    • 0.1–1 V is common in the literature; if the voltage is too low, the current is too small and noise makes up a larger share.

  8. Set the parameters in Experiment › E Physical Electrochemistry › Chronoamperometry (field names may differ in your software).

    • Pre-step: 0 V, about 30 s.
    • Step 1: ΔV, e.g., 0.100 V; time 7200–36000 s (2–10 h), long enough for the current to reach steady state.
    • Step 2: 0 V, for about the same time as Step 1, to return the cell to its initial state.
    • Sample Period 5–10 s; I/E Range Auto; Area 1 cm², to read the raw current.
    • Set the voltage vs Eref: an SS–SS cell stores no energy, so ΔV is then the actual voltage across the cell. Do this only for SS–SS cells; charged cells must use vs Eoc.
    • Give Output File a distinguishable name, e.g., S3-1_60C_DCpol_0p1V.DTA.

  9. Click OK to run. The yellow Cell On light stays on; do not touch the leads or the shield during the hours of the run. Manual Ch 7, 9

    • After the run, plot |I| vs time in Echem Analyst 2 with a logarithmic y-axis.
    • Steady state: the current changes by less than about 1–2% over the last 1 h; take the average of the final segment as Iss. General method
    • Current still falling: increase Step 1 Time and measure again.
    • Sudden jumps in current: check whether someone touched the leads or the temperature fluctuated.

  10. Before the next voltage, rest the cell at 0 V for at least as long as the polarization, so the ion distribution recovers. General method

    • Go from low to high voltage; higher voltages may decompose the sample, so do them last.
    • You can also use a new cell for each voltage, but cell-to-cell differences then enter the result.

  11. After all voltages are done, measure EIS again; Rb should be essentially the same as in step 6. General method

    • Rb changed noticeably: the sample decomposed or was compressed during polarization, or a short circuit developed; this data set is not reliable.
    • After disassembly, check the film for discoloration, blistering, or holes.

  12. Subtract the blank current, plot Iss against ΔV, and calculate the electronic conductivity from the slope of a line through the origin. General method

    • σe = (L / A) × slope, with the slope in A/V.
    • The points should fall on a straight line through the origin; if a high-voltage point bends upward, an electrochemical reaction has occurred, so do not use that point.
    • Ionic transference number: tion = σion / (σion + σe), with σion from the EIS in step 6.
    • In the report, state ΔV, polarization time, temperature, L and A, blank current, and Rb before and after polarization. The calculator below does this directly.

12 · Li transference number

Li+ transference number (Li | electrolyte | Li)

On a Li symmetric cell, run EIS → 10 mV potentiostatic polarization to steady state → EIS (Bruce–Vincent method), then calculate t+ from the initial and steady-state currents and the interfacial resistances before and after polarization. Stainless steel blocks Li+ ions, so an SS | SS cell cannot measure t+. The method itself is a general electrochemical method.

t+ = Iss(ΔV − I0R0) / [I0(ΔV − IssRss)]
ΔV
Polarization voltage, usually 10 mV
I0, Iss
Initial and steady-state current
R0, Rss
Interfacial resistance before and after polarization

  1. Assemble Li / electrolyte / Li in the glovebox, bring it to the test temperature (often 60–80 °C for PEO-based systems), and wait for the interface to stabilize before starting. General method

    • Make the film one size larger than the Li foil to prevent edge shorts; if the lithium surface has an oxide layer, scrape it gently until shiny.
    • Measure EIS at intervals and start only when the interfacial semicircle no longer changes noticeably. If you measure while the interface is still changing, t+ will be inaccurate.

  2. EIS before polarization: Experiment › G Electrochemical Impedance › Potentiostatic EIS, 1 MHz → 0.1 Hz, AC 5–10 mV rms (no larger than ΔV), DC 0 V vs Eoc. General method

    • In Echem Analyst 2, fit with Rs + (Rb ∥ CPE) + (Ri ∥ CPE) to get the bulk resistance Rb,0 and the interfacial resistance R0.
    • R0 is the interfacial resistance and does not include the bulk resistance.

  3. Potentiostatic polarization: Experiment › E Physical Electrochemistry › Chronoamperometry, Step 1 = 0.010 V vs Eoc, 1–5 h. Field names may differ in your software.

    • The open-circuit voltage of a Li symmetric cell is close to 0, so vs Eoc and vs Eref differ very little, but use vs Eoc consistently.
    • Set Max Current no smaller than ΔV / (Rb,0 + R0), and set IRComp to None.
    • EIS and polarization can be chained into one sequence with Experiment › Sequence Wizard.

  4. The current decays from its initial value I0 to the steady state Iss. When it changes by less than about 1% over 30–60 min, take the average of the final segment as Iss. General method

    • I0 can be taken from the start of the curve, but it is affected by double-layer charging and the sampling time; calculating I0 = ΔV / (Rb,0 + R0) is more reliable, and the two should be close.
    • The yellow Cell On light is on; do not touch the leads during the hours of the run.

  5. Measure EIS immediately after polarization: turn Init. Delay off or set it very short, fit with the same equivalent circuit, and get Rss. General method

    • Alternatively, measure while holding the 10 mV bias (DC = 0.010 V vs Eref, which is safe for a Li symmetric cell). Choose one approach and state it in the report.

  6. Calculate t+ from the formula, check that the result is reasonable, and average over 2–3 cells. The calculator below does this directly. General method

    • t+ greater than 1 or less than 0, or Iss greater than I0: the interface changed too much during the measurement, there is a short, or the polarization time was too short.
    • For concentrated polymer electrolytes, the measured value is an apparent transference number; state the method, temperature, and ΔV in the report.

13 · Stability window

Electrochemical stability window (SS | electrolyte | Li)

With Li as both counter and reference electrode, sweep slowly from the open-circuit potential to high potential. The potential where the current clearly rises is the oxidation limit (vs Li/Li+). On the reduction side, use cyclic voltammetry to look at Li plating and stripping. Use a new cell for each sweep. The method itself is a general electrochemical method.

  1. Assemble SS / electrolyte / Li with the lithium on the negative cap side. Wiring: green + blue to the stainless steel, white + red + orange to the lithium; vs Eref in the software then means vs Li/Li+. Manual Ch 6

    • After assembly, record the open-circuit voltage and let the cell rest at the test temperature.
    • Use a new cell for each scan: the scanned electrolyte has already decomposed.

  2. Oxidation side: Experiment › E Physical Electrochemistry › Linear Sweep Voltammetry, scanning slowly from the open-circuit potential to high potential. Field names may differ in your software. General method

    • Scan rate 0.1–1 mV/s; set the final potential for the system, e.g., 6.0 V vs Li.
    • This is a charged half cell: set the initial potential vs Eoc, and the final potential vs Eref, which here is the potential vs lithium.

  3. Define the criterion before reading the limit: e.g., the potential at which the current density exceeds 10 µA/cm², or the intersection of the tangents to the baseline and the rising segment. General method

    • Current density = current ÷ contact area between the stainless steel and the electrolyte.
    • In the report, state the scan rate, temperature, working electrode, and criterion; the slower the scan, the more conservative the limit.
    • A more sensitive approach: hold the potential at candidate values (E › Chronoamperometry) and watch the long-term current.

  4. Reduction side: with another new SS / electrolyte / Li cell, run cyclic voltammetry (E › Cyclic Voltammetry), cycling a few times between about −0.5 V and 3 V vs Li. General method

    • The reduction peak near 0 V is lithium plating on stainless steel, and the oxidation peak on the reverse scan is lithium stripping; this is not electrolyte decomposition.
    • The ratio of plating to stripping charge reflects the reversibility of lithium plating.
14 · Li symmetric cycling

Li symmetric cycling and critical current density

Pass a constant current through Li | electrolyte | Li, alternating between positive and negative, and check whether the voltage plateau stays stable. Raise the current density step by step; the step at which the voltage suddenly drops to near 0 is the critical current density (CCD). The method itself is a general electrochemical method.

  1. Assemble Li / electrolyte / Li and let it rest until EIS is stable. Convert current density to current: I = j × A, with A taken as the Li foil area. General method

    • Program: the charge/discharge cycling program under Experiment › J Electrochemical Energy (check the name in the menu); without this module, use E › Chronopotentiometry and chain alternating +I and −I steps into a sequence in Sequence Wizard.
    • Common starting values: 0.05–0.1 mA/cm², 0.5–1 h per half cycle, voltage limits of e.g. ±1 V; adjust for your system.
    • There is only one channel: long cycling of several days or more is better done on a battery tester.

  2. A normal curve: the voltage alternates between positive and negative, with the same plateau height in every half cycle. The plateau height is the overpotential of both sides plus the iR drop. General method

    • Report the current density, time per half cycle, temperature, and how the plateau voltage changes with cycling.

  3. Two kinds of anomaly: plateaus that grow gradually indicate a deteriorating interface; a voltage that suddenly drops and becomes noisy indicates a short circuit caused by lithium dendrites. Record the time of the short and stop. General method

    • Measure EIS once before and once after cycling, and compare the interfacial resistance.
    • After the test, disassemble the cell in the glovebox, check the lithium surface for dendrites and discoloration, and take photos for the record.

  4. Critical current density (CCD): with a new cell, start at 0.05 mA/cm² and increase by 0.05 mA/cm² each cycle (e.g., 30 min per half cycle). The step at which the voltage suddenly drops to near 0 is the CCD. General method

    • Report the step before the short and the step at which the short occurred, along with the temperature and time per half cycle.
    • CCD usually increases with temperature, so results at different temperatures cannot be compared directly.

18 · After the test

After testing a coin cell

From removing the cell and recording the data to opening the case, inspecting the electrolyte film, sorting the waste and cleaning the work area, each step has a schematic. The instrument points follow the manual; the rest is general lab practice. For waste disposal, your institution's safety rules take precedence.

  1. Wait until the experiment ends and the yellow Cell On light goes off, then release the holder or alligator clips and remove the cell. Manual Ch 7

    • Charged half cells and full cells: keep the removed clips apart so they do not touch each other or the other terminal of the cell.
    • If you are measuring another cell next, leave the leads on the holder.

  2. Confirm the .DTA file has been saved, and record the cell information so that each cell matches its data.

    • Record the cell number, sample and batch, L and A, temperature (heating or cooling segment), and file name; for full cells, also record the open-circuit voltage.
    • Measure twice at the same temperature; the spectra should essentially overlap. If they do not, the system has not stabilized yet or the geometry is changing. General method
    • Write the number on the case with a marker so you can match the parts after disassembly.

  3. Inspect the cell before disassembly: continue only if it is flat, clean, and not warm. General method

    • Do not disassemble a cell that is swollen, leaking, has white crystals at the edge, or is warm. Isolate it in a fireproof container (e.g., a metal can filled with sand) and report it to the lab supervisor.

  4. Decide how to handle the cell according to its type. General method

    • SS | electrolyte | SS: stores no energy and contains no lithium metal, so it can be disassembled directly; if the sample contains a lithium salt or is moisture-sensitive, still disassemble it in the glovebox.
    • Li | electrolyte | Li: contains lithium metal. Disassemble in the glovebox and handle the Li foil according to your group's procedures.
    • Half cells and full cells: discharge first, e.g., galvanostatically to the cutoff voltage, or by the method your institution specifies; do not discharge by shorting with a wire. After discharge, wrap both terminals with insulating tape.

  5. Open the case: pry open the crimped rim with a dedicated decrimper or insulated pliers, and remove the negative cap, spring, and spacers. General method

    • Wear safety glasses and gloves. Do not let the tool touch the negative cap and positive case at the same time, or the cell will short through the tool.
    • Disassemble cells containing lithium metal or LiPF6-type electrolytes in the glovebox: LiPF6 decomposes in contact with water and produces HF.

  6. Inspect the electrolyte film and remeasure the thickness L. General method

    • Check whether the film has flowed beyond the spacer or has holes; for Li symmetric cells, also check the lithium surface for dendrites or discoloration. Take photos for the record.
    • If the thickness after disassembly differs noticeably from that before assembly, the film was compressed during the measurement and this batch of data should be used with caution; for soft electrolytes, switch to a PTFE ring spacer.

  7. Sort the removed parts for disposal. Follow your institution's EHS (environmental health and safety) rules for sorting and handover. General method

    • Battery waste: discharged cells with both terminals taped.
    • Chemical waste: electrolyte films, separators, and wipes wet with electrolyte.
    • Lithium metal: do not put it in regular trash or let it contact water (lithium reacts violently with water, releasing hydrogen). Passivate it according to the procedure before disposal. One common method is to let small amounts of lithium react slowly with isopropanol in a fume hood; this also releases hydrogen, so keep away from ignition sources, and do it only where the procedure allows and under supervision.
    • Reusable: if your group allows, clean SS spacers with solvent, dry them, and reuse them; cases, caps, and springs are generally single-use.

  8. Clean the holder and workstation. General method

    • Wipe the holder contacts clean with a lint-free wipe dampened with isopropanol: electrolyte residue corrodes the contacts and increases contact resistance.
    • Hang the cell cable leads separately so the metal ends do not touch each other.
    • If the instrument will not be used again that day, shut it down following the operating SOP.
