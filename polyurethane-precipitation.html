Polyurethane Precipitation Bench

Turn the viscous TPU solution from the synthesis into a clean, dry polymer. Dilute it, pour it into a stirred non-solvent, wash out the DMAc, filter, dry to constant weight, then check the result by TGA and ¹H NMR. Every choice changes how the polymer comes out.

    What a good precipitation looks like

    Check each stage against these signs. If something looks different, find it in the troubleshooting table below.

    While pouring

    • The stream turns opaque white the moment it hits the liquid.
    • It draws out into continuous threads, like cotton or thin noodles, that wind onto the stir bar or a glass rod.
    • The liquid stays clear, or at most slightly hazy. It never turns milky.

    In the beaker

    • The fibres are white to cream (MDI grades are slightly off-white), firm and not tacky.
    • Squeezed, they spring back and do not fuse together or stick to the glass.
    • They settle fast when stirring stops, under a clear supernatant.

    On the filter

    • Suction pulls the liquid through in seconds.
    • The cake is fluffy and fibrous, not a paste.
    • After washing there is no fishy, amine-like DMAc smell.

    After drying

    • Fluffy white fibres or crumbs that stretch and snap back.
    • Constant weight, and 90–98 % of the theoretical solids.
    • Residual DMAc below 0.5 wt % by NMR, or below 0.1 wt % for cell or biomedical work.
    • It redissolves completely in DMAc to a clear solution with no gel specks.

    Troubleshooting

    What you seeLikely causeWhat to do
    White continuous fibres, clear liquidGood molecular weight, thin solution, strong stirringNothing. Wash it 2–3 times and dry it.
    One big lump, sticky insideSolution too concentrated, poured too fast, or weak stirringDilute to 10–15 wt %, stir harder, pour slower. Cut the lump into small pieces and soak it longer.
    Soft, sticky gum instead of fibresLow molecular weight, or too little non-solvent so the bath holds too much DMAcUse ice-cold non-solvent and at least 5–10 volumes. A low-Mn batch stays gummy; the fix is in the synthesis.
    Milky dispersion that will not settle or filterVery low molecular weight, or particles too fineCentrifuge, or add a little NaCl to flocculate the particles.
    Solution already a gel in the flaskCrosslinking, for example allophanate from overheatingIt cannot be precipitated. The batch is lost.
    Dry polymer still smells of DMAc, or NMR shows 2.94 / 2.78 ppm singletsToo few washes, or lumps too thick to extractWash more instead of drying longer. DMAc binds to urethane groups and leaves the solid only slowly in the oven.

    Safety, waste and the model

    Safety

    • DMAc and DMF pass through skin and are reproductive toxicants. Nitrile gloves give only minutes of protection against them. Use butyl-rubber gloves for this work, or double nitrile changed at once after any splash.
    • Work in a fume hood. Methanol is flammable and toxic. Keep it away from the hotplate.
    • Vent the vacuum oven slowly, and never put a methanol-wet sample into a hot oven that is not under vacuum.

    Waste

    • The bath and wash liquids contain DMAc and traces of tin catalyst. Collect them as aqueous waste with organic solvent. Do not pour them down the drain.
    • Methanol washes go to non-halogenated organic waste.
    • The readouts track how much liquid waste you generate. More washes mean cleaner polymer and more waste.

    How the simulation works

    • Solution viscosity uses the Martin equation with a Mark–Houwink estimate of intrinsic viscosity, the same model as the synthesis bench.
    • The precipitate's form comes from a rule-based score built on viscosity, pour rate, stirring, molecular weight and how much DMAc ends up in the bath.
    • Washing is first-order exchange toward a partition equilibrium (K = 2), with a time constant that grows steeply with fibre or lump thickness.
    • Drying is first-order evaporation for each volatile. The temperature and vacuum factors are illustrative values, not measured data.
